SilentCKD
For GP clinics and care teams

1 in 5 adults with diabetes or high blood pressure has kidney damage. Most don't know.

SilentCKD reads a routine check-up record and answers one question: is this patient likely to have hidden kidney damage, and should they be first in line for a urine test?

"Kidney damage" here means protein leaking into the urine, measured by a urine ACR of 30 or more. It is often the first measurable sign of chronic kidney disease, and the earliest point at which a clinic can act.

Does it work?

Tested on two separate groups of people, then followed for 10 years

The problem

Kidney damage is silent, and the usual blood test misses most of it

85 in 100

people with kidney damage have never been told they have kidney disease.

72 in 100

people with kidney damage have a normal kidney blood test. Only a urine test shows it.

1 in 3

people with kidney damage have no diabetes and a normal blood test, so they are the least likely to be tested.

4 in 100

people with high blood pressure get the urine test within two years. With diabetes, it's 35 in 100. Source

The urine test (called ACR) is cheap and quick but often skipped. Found early, kidney damage can be slowed with treatment. The first three figures come from 13,506 adults with diabetes or high blood pressure in a US national health survey (NHANES).

How it works

Three steps, using what's already on file

Use what's already on file

Age, blood pressure, blood sugar, a kidney blood test and a few yes/no questions. Blanks are fine.

2

Get a direct answer

Likely, Possible or Unlikely, shown as "X in 100 people with a record like this have kidney damage".

3

See why, and what to review

The main reasons in plain words, and points for the doctor to review. Never an order.

Safety by design

A prompt for a test, not a diagnosis

  • The only action it suggests is a urine test, or a doctor's review if the kidney blood test is low. A doctor makes every decision.
  • It shows its reasons, so staff can question any answer.
  • It warns when a patient is unusual compared with the people it learned from.
  • It never uses race. Kidney function uses the 2021 race-free equation.
  • Everything runs in your browser. No patient data leaves the page.
Honest limits

What it can't do

  • It finds damage that is already there. We could follow patients for deaths over 10 years, but not for new kidney disease, so it isn't proven to predict that.
  • It has only been tested on US survey data, not on a real hospital's records. A clinic should check it on its own patients first.
  • "Kidney damage" here means one raised urine result. Doctors confirm with a repeat test.
  • It is weaker for people with high blood pressure only: it catches 67 in 100 of their kidney damage, against 93 in 100 for people with diabetes.
  • It decides who to test first. It doesn't replace the yearly urine test guidelines already recommend for diabetes.

What should my file look like?

A spreadsheet saved as CSV, one patient per row. Only age and sex are required; every other column helps. Column names are matched automatically (for example "SBP", "Systolic BP" and "systolic" all work), and common unit mix-ups, such as creatinine in µmol/L, are fixed for you.

patient_id, age, sex, systolic_bp, diastolic_bp, diagnosed_diabetes, diabetes_years, hba1c_percent, on_bp_meds, heart_disease_or_stroke, current_smoker, creatinine_mg_dl, …

Got health-record exports instead?

You can also upload FHIR R4 files, the standard format hospital record systems export (.json or .ndjson, one or many files). SilentCKD pulls out the blood pressure, lab results, diagnoses and medicines, and leaves out anyone who is under 20, has died, or has neither diabetes nor high blood pressure. Is it accurate and fair?

We built it on 10,923 adults from a US national health survey (2011–2020), then tested it on 2,583 newer adults from 2021–2023 whose data was never used to train it. Here's how it did.

What each answer means

Of 100 patients who got each answer, how many really had kidney damage?

Likely means a chance of 50 in 100 or more. Possible means about 13 to 49 in 100 (from about 9 in 100 for under 45s). Unlikely is anything lower. The bar for Possible is low on purpose: the urine test is cheap, and at this bar about 8 in 10 people with kidney damage get flagged.

Fairness

Does it work as well for everyone?

No race in the model

Race is never an input. When we tried adding it, accuracy barely changed (less than 0.3%), so it isn't needed.

Race-free kidney test

We use the 2021 kidney equation with no race adjustment. The old one hid low kidney function in 158 Black patients in our data.

People with kidney damage who were caught, by group

Caught: of 100 people in the group with kidney damage, how many got Likely or Possible. Range: where the true figure probably lies. False alarms: of 100 people in the group without kidney damage, how many were flagged anyway.

What is still weak: it catches the fewest cases in people with high blood pressure only, and false alarms are high for people with diabetes, who should be tested every year anyway. Small groups, such as the 32 Asian patients with kidney damage, have wide ranges, so their numbers are less certain.

Technical details for reviewers

Data

NHANES 2011–2023 (5 survey cycles, CDC public data). Adults 20+ with diabetes (diagnosed or HbA1c ≥6.5%) or hypertension (diagnosed, treated, or ≥140/90), with urine ACR and serum creatinine; pregnant participants excluded. n = 13,506. Outcome: albuminuria, urine ACR ≥30 mg/g. No urine values are used as inputs.

Model

XGBoost, 400 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.03, monotone constraints (risk can only rise with blood pressure, HbA1c, diabetes years and age, and only fall with eGFR). 25 inputs, no race. Explanations are exact TreeSHAP values computed in the browser (matching XGBoost to 1e-6), grouped into plain-language topics. Answers: Likely at 50% or more; Possible at 13.0% or more (8.6% under age 45). Cut-off values were fitted on development data, but the simplified answer rules were chosen after reviewing 2021–2023 results, so 2021–2023 answer-level results are slightly optimistic; the locked test below is the clean estimate. Whatever the score, an eGFR below 60 prompts a doctor's review. "What if" options that would raise the chance are hidden, because BMI and smoking have no monotone constraint and such comparisons are artefacts.

Short-form model

Used when more than 2 of 9 optional measures are blank. 11 inputs, trained with blood pressure, HbA1c and creatinine randomly blanked. AUROC 0.749 on 2021–2023 (0.696 with no blood pressure, HbA1c or creatinine), predicted prevalence 19.8% vs 19.0% observed.

Results on 2021–2023 (temporal hold-out, n = 2,583)

  • AUROC 0.754 (95% CI 0.730–0.778); survey-weighted 0.765
  • Logistic regression with the same inputs 0.728; XGBoost gain 0.026 (paired bootstrap 95% CI 0.015–0.037)
  • AUPRC 0.478 (prevalence 0.19); Brier score 0.129
  • Calibration slope 0.97, intercept −0.07
  • Same number of tests as diabetes-first (886): finds 63.4% of cases vs 54.5%
  • Single cut-off 0.130: sensitivity 0.79, specificity 0.55, PPV 0.29
  • Unusual-record warning: mean distance to 100 nearest of 6,000 reference patients above the 99th percentile

Locked external test, 2007–2010 (n = 2,469)

  • Models and answer rules frozen before this data was run; these cycles were used for nothing else
  • AUROC 0.749 (95% CI 0.723–0.774); calibration slope 0.95, intercept −0.17
  • This resolves the known limit of the 2021–2023 answer-level numbers: those rules were adjusted after viewing that test set

Linked mortality through 2019 (n = 2,466, 555 deaths, median 9.8 y)

  • Cox models adjusted for age and sex. Likely vs Unlikely HR 4.2 (3.15–5.59); Possible 1.72 (1.38–2.15)
  • ACR <30 and eGFR ≥60 today (n = 1,765): flagged vs Unlikely, heart, stroke, diabetes or kidney death HR 2.30 (1.42–3.74)
  • Unweighted. Only 7 kidney deaths, so no kidney-specific claim
